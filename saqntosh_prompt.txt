Paper I am presenting:
“Efficient Multilingual Spam Detection on Resource-Constrained Devices: A Comparative Analysis of QLoRA Fine-Tuning of Gemma 3, Qwen 3, and Llama 3.2 Models.”

The paper was published in Complex & Intelligent Systems in 2026.

The problem:
Modern spam affects many languages, but strong language models are often too large for small devices. Real multilingual data is also difficult and privacy-sensitive to collect. The paper asks whether compact language models can achieve useful multilingual performance while using less memory.

Research questions:
1. Which compact multilingual model performs best after the same QLoRA training process?
2. How much performance is lost when the models are reduced from 16-bit to 4-bit?
3. Can QLoRA recover or improve that performance?
4. Can the models fit within practical memory limits, and can synthetic translation address multilingual data scarcity?

Dataset and quality checks:
- Started with the public Enron email corpus.
- Used Aya-101 to create a balanced dataset across 21 languages.
- Created 42,000 records: 1,000 spam and 1,000 non-spam messages per language.
- Used an 80:20 training and validation split.
- Checked a sample of 30 spam and 30 non-spam messages per language for meaning, tone, and label quality.

Experimental design:
- Compared Gemma 3-4B, Qwen 3-4B, and Llama 3.2-3B.
- Tested each model in a 16-bit baseline, a 4-bit baseline, and a 4-bit QLoRA fine-tuned stage.
- Used the same training setup for a fair comparison.
- Trained on one NVIDIA T4 GPU.
- Measured accuracy, precision, recall, F1, confusion matrices, language-level performance, and memory use.

Main results:
- Gemma reached 90% accuracy and 0.88 F1.
- Qwen reached 84% accuracy and 0.82 F1.
- Llama reached 70% accuracy and 0.69 F1.
- Quantization caused only a small initial accuracy drop.
- QLoRA then improved every model beyond its 16-bit baseline.
- Quantization reduced memory use by more than half.
- Qwen used about 3.32 GB and Llama about 2.24 GB, while Gemma used about 4.56 GB.

What the results mean:
The strongest model was not the smallest one. Gemma gave the best accuracy, Qwen offered the strongest balance, and Llama had the smallest memory footprint. This shows that model quality and deployment cost should be studied together.

The language-level analysis also showed that one average score can hide weak performance in particular languages. This is important because real health models can also perform differently across people, groups, devices, and settings.

Important limitations:
- The results use one synthetic corpus.
- The evaluation uses a validation split rather than a completely independent real-world test set.
- The study classifies email body text only.
- Native speakers should provide deeper language-quality checks.
- Future work should test real multilingual data, email headers, security signals, additional models, and statistical significance.
